 Uncertainty-Based Risk Ordering and Objective-Specific Ligand Ranking for Metal–Ligand Selectivity

 Abstract

Machine-learning models for metal–ligand selectivity are usually assessed with global regression metrics and molecular-similarity applicability domains, whereas experimental use requires ranking ligands for one competing-metal pair at a time. Using heterogeneous literature measurements from the Critical Materials Institute M4 collection, we model pairwise selectivity ($\Delta\log K$) directly under ligand-disjoint validation, which tests generalization to genuinely unseen ligands, and score it with pair-equal metrics; a heteroscedastic multilayer-perceptron ensemble is fixed before the downstream analyses and held constant across all of them. Its predictive uncertainty orders held-out error far more effectively than fingerprint, descriptor-space and learned-representation distances or a pair-support count, and several of those distances show no positive association with held-out error at all. At 50% retention, uncertainty ranking reduces all-pair mean-of-pairs root mean squared error (RMSE) from $2.078 \pm 0.130$ to $0.730 \pm 0.084$, against $1.586 \pm 0.198$ for the leading registered descriptor-space distance, and it remains predictive of error after controlling for predicted selectivity magnitude. Retrospective pair-specific ranking then shows that *how* uncertainty enters a ranking rule matters more than whether it does. A standardized threshold margin, which divides the predicted distance past the event threshold by the predictive standard deviation $\sigma_{\mathrm{total}}$, gives the best event recovery on both a high-selectivity and a near-zero objective, and matches or exceeds the value-only baseline on every event metric but one, where it trails by $0.001$. Subtracting $\sigma_{\mathrm{total}}$ as a lower confidence bound instead helps only on the near-zero objective and degrades high-selectivity recovery monotonically, until at the strongest penalty it finds a qualifying ligand less often than random ranking. Risk ordering calibrated against held-out outcomes is therefore a far better applicability domain than molecular distance alone (fold-mean Spearman $\rho$ with absolute residual $0.648 \pm 0.020$, against $0.250 \pm 0.078$ for the best distance tested).

 1. Introduction

Machine-learning models for molecular property prediction are often evaluated under data partitions that are substantially easier than the intended deployment problem. In a random row split, closely related molecules, repeated ligand scaffolds, or even the same ligand can occur in both the training and test partitions. The resulting estimate may therefore measure interpolation within a densely sampled chemical neighborhood rather than prediction for a genuinely new chemical structure. This distinction is particularly important for small and heterogeneous chemical datasets, where a limited number of scaffolds or experimental sources can dominate the apparent performance of a model.

The cheminformatics community has responded by developing and adopting evaluation protocols that make structural novelty more explicit. Scaffold splits, similarity- or cluster-based partitions, temporal splits, and applicability-domain analyses are now commonly used alongside random splits to quantify how performance changes as the test set becomes less similar to the training set.<sup>1–3</sup> Fingerprint-based Tanimoto similarity is frequently used to characterize this structural relationship because it provides an interpretable, model-independent measure of the nearest training examples.<sup>4</sup> However, structural similarity is not equivalent to prediction reliability. Similar molecules can exhibit activity cliffs, condition-dependent behavior, or different measurement noise, whereas dissimilarity may reflect only one component of the factors that determine prediction error.<sup>5,6</sup> Consequently, a structural distance should be evaluated as an empirical predictor of held-out error rather than assumed to define a universal applicability-domain boundary.

These issues become more complex when the modeled property depends not only on molecular structure but also on chemical context. A large fraction of early cheminformatics work was formulated as a structure–property problem in which a molecular representation was mapped to a measured endpoint. Many practical chemical tasks, however, are more accurately represented as structure-plus-context problems. The relevant inputs may include assay conditions, solvent or medium, temperature, pH, ionic strength, concentration, protein or cell-line identity, reaction partners, or other variables that determine the chemical environment in which the property is observed. In such settings, the same ligand can have different measured properties under different experimental conditions, and a model based only on molecular structure is not designed to distinguish these cases.

Metal–ligand chemistry provides a particularly clear example of this transition. Properties of a coordination complex depend on the ligand, the metal element, oxidation state, charge, solvent or electrolyte, temperature, and other formulation variables. Recent resources for metal-containing compounds illustrate the increasing use of multimodal chemical records that combine molecular or complex representations with metal identity and biological or experimental context. For example, large-scale transition-metal cytotoxicity resources organize measurements according to both metal-containing compounds and biological test systems,<sup>7</sup> while quantum-chemical resources such as tmQM demonstrate the need for representations that explicitly encode metal-containing molecular structures.<sup>8</sup> These examples differ in endpoint and data-generation process, but they share an important modeling principle: metal-containing chemistry cannot generally be reduced to an organic ligand fingerprint without loss of relevant information.

Metal–ligand stability prediction is the case in point, and these dependencies are central to metal extraction and selective separation, where rare-earth and other technologically important metals often have similar chemical properties. The practical objective is therefore usually selective rather than absolute binding: identifying ligands that preferentially bind one metal over a competing metal under comparable conditions. Such selectivity is important for solvent extraction, metal recovery, analytical separations, and related applications.

This problem is well suited to machine learning because the experimental search space spans many ligands, metal pairs, and chemical environments, while exhaustive experimental screening is costly. A model that combines ligand structure with metal and condition descriptors could reduce this search space and prioritize candidates for experimental testing. At the same time, metal–ligand stability data provide a demanding test of cheminformatics methodology: literature measurements are heterogeneous, support is uneven across metal pairs, and the intended use is often to rank a small number of candidates rather than to optimize average regression accuracy over the full dataset.

A major contribution in this area was made by the Critical Materials Institute (CMI), whose models for metal–ligand binding were trained on a curated literature collection of protonation and stability constants and released together with their weights and data splits.<sup>9</sup> An earlier study from the same group had already used predicted stability constants to investigate ligand design for optimal metal-ion selectivity.<sup>10</sup> Together this work combined ligand or complex representations with metal and experimental-condition descriptors, established the feasibility of applying machine learning to heterogeneous metal–ligand stability data, and demonstrated the value of integrating chemical context into the prediction task. Because the CMI models, their training and test files, and their five-fold checkpoints are openly available,<sup>9</sup> that benchmark can be audited directly, and we use it below as the reference point for what a published split does and does not measure.

The study also illustrates a broader issue concerning external validation. In this domain, chemical novelty can arise from a new ligand, an underrepresented metal species or pair, a different experimental medium, or a new combination of these factors. A structural-similarity-based external set therefore probes a different form of generalization from a ligand-disjoint, metal-pair-disjoint, or condition-disjoint evaluation. Determining whether an applicability-domain definition reflects the type of novelty encountered in practice is consequently an important part of model assessment. Structural distance remains a useful and interpretable reference, but it may not capture prediction risk caused by sparse metal-pair support, unusual coordination contexts, or variability in the underlying measurements.

Active-learning and sequential-design studies provide a complementary framework for addressing this problem.<sup>11</sup> In these workflows, models are used not only to predict properties but also to decide which compounds should be measured next. Uncertainty-quantification (UQ) approaches—including deep ensembles,<sup>12</sup> Monte Carlo dropout,<sup>13</sup> evidential models,<sup>14,15</sup> Gaussian-process models,<sup>16,17</sup> and heteroscedastic regression<sup>18</sup>—attach a risk estimate to individual predictions, and have been benchmarked directly for molecular property prediction.<sup>19</sup> These estimates can be used to identify poorly supported candidates, balance exploration and exploitation, or restrict experimental campaigns to predictions with lower expected error. Their practical value, however, should be tested against classical molecular-distance measures and against held-out errors rather than assumed from the form of the uncertainty estimate alone.

For metal extraction, this evaluation should focus on pair-specific selectivity rather than only on bulk regression statistics. A campaign may seek ligands that exceed a high-selectivity threshold, candidates that show little preference between two metals, or the strongest available candidate for a particular pair. These objectives are not interchangeable, and the ranking strategy that best recovers any qualifying ligand may not identify the candidate with the greatest achievable selectivity. Accordingly, the present work first establishes what a ligand-disjoint validation regime measures that a published random-style assignment does not, then investigates direct modeling of metal-pair selectivity, compares uncertainty-based and classical domain-risk measures as predictors of held-out error, and evaluates objective-specific ligand ranking under held-out, prospective-style conditions. The central question is whether these approaches can provide useful control of prediction risk and practical guidance about which ligand predictions are sufficiently reliable to support the next experiment.

 2. Methods

The analyses below answer different questions and should not be read as one common protocol. Historical CMI reproduction audits the published official assignment. Ligand-disjoint DS1, the absolute-binding dataset, benchmarks $\log K$ prediction. Ligand-disjoint DS2, the pairwise-selectivity dataset, benchmarks direct $\Delta\log K$ prediction. Downstream retention and risk ordering use the fixed DS2 *workhorse*: the single uncertainty-bearing DS2 model chosen before those analyses begin and held constant across them, so that the predictor does not change as the evaluation objective changes. The term denotes that operational role and carries no claim of unique or universal superiority. Pair acquisition is a retrospective held-out ranking simulation. Ad hoc DS1 differencing is a comparator; Section 3.2 reports how it compares with direct DS2 modeling and which is carried forward. Feature representations (RDKit tabular descriptors;<sup>20</sup> Chemprop directed message-passing neural network, D-MPNN, graphs<sup>21,22</sup>) are inputs to these datasets; they are not themselves DS1 or DS2.

All modeling used Python 3.11 with RDKit 2023.09.6,<sup>20</sup> Chemprop 1.6.1,<sup>22</sup> and scikit-learn<sup>23</sup> for grouped splitting, feature scaling, and shrinkage covariance estimation.

 2.1 Literature M4 and task-specific datasets

Literature M4 is the heterogeneous CMI collection of reported metal–ligand binding measurements. We derived two task-specific datasets from the normalized collection: DS1, the absolute-binding dataset for predicting $\log K$, and DS2, the pairwise-selectivity dataset for predicting $\Delta\log K$. DS1 and DS2 are therefore not alternative feature representations of identical rows; they have different targets, example-construction rules, and modeling scopes.

DS1 contains 43,388 records from 4,347 unique ligands, including 32,029 metal-complex records and 11,359 protonation records. DS2 is constructed only from metal-complex records and contains 88,525 ligand-level pairwise rows, 2,503 species-level pairs, and 1,848 unordered element pairs. Protonation records do not enter DS2. The primary DS2 benchmark is the full-data all-pair scope. Dataset composition, feature dimensions, and fold sizes are in Tables S1.1–S1.4.

 2.1.1 Normalization and exclusions

Ligand structures, metal identity, oxidation state, and experimental-condition fields were normalized before modeling. The ligand was represented by the largest organic fragment by heavy-atom count, or otherwise the largest non-metal fragment. Predictor fields included ligand charge, complex charge, metal oxidation state, temperature, ionic strength, solvent molecular weight, and solvent density. Electrolyte identity was decoded from solvent molecular weight and density and was used for matching and grouping, but not as a direct DS2 predictor. Records with failed ligand parsing, inconsistent metal identity, missing required condition fields, or unresolved electrolyte identity were quarantined before dataset construction; quarantine is a hard exclusion, not imputation. The quarantine categories and the CMI external-file treatment (398 records, 40 ligands; excluded from DS1/DS2 production modeling) are described in Supplementary Section S1.

 2.1.2 DS1 construction

DS1 retains the reported absolute $\log K$ for each normalized measurement. Under the selected `train_with_H` policy, protonation records are retained in the DS1 modeling table, although the headline DS1 accuracy results are reported on held-out metal-complex records. Repeated measurements are not collapsed before DS1 training; therefore, DS1 preserves disagreement among nominally matching literature observations.

 2.1.3 DS2 construction

DS2 is formed in two stages. First, metal-complex records are grouped by ligand identity, metal element, metal oxidation state, temperature, ionic strength, and electrolyte identity. Repeated $\log K$ values within each species-and-condition group are replaced by their median. Second, two species-level records are paired when they share the ligand and the required non-metal experimental conditions. The ordered difference between their aggregated values defines the DS2 target:

$$
\Delta\log K(X,Y,L)=\log K(X,L)-\log K(Y,L).
$$

Reversing the metal order reverses the sign:

$$
\Delta\log K(Y,X,L)=-\Delta\log K(X,Y,L).
$$

A species-level pair preserves the element and oxidation state of both metals. Consequently, species-level pairs and unordered element pairs are different units: the 2,503 species-level pairs should not be interpreted as 2,503 unique element pairs. Canonical and reversed high-selectivity directions are therefore separate ranking tasks in the acquisition analysis, even though they arise from the same underlying metal-pair relationship.

 2.1.4 Within-condition disagreement

Nominally matching literature measurements can disagree substantially. We treat this disagreement as descriptive internal variability rather than as a separately estimated laboratory-noise variance. Because a replicate-spread analysis is not identifiable under the ligand-disjoint design, no independent experimental-noise variance is estimated. The predictive uncertainty reported below should therefore be interpreted as a composite prediction-risk signal, not as a direct laboratory-noise estimate. The composite reading is used consistently throughout. Numerical within-condition examples are in Supplementary Section S1.

 2.2 Validation regime

We distinguish the historical CMI assignment from the primary ligand-disjoint evaluation. The historical assignment is reproduced only to characterize the published benchmark and its ligand-overlap structure. The primary study uses five-fold ligand-disjoint grouped cross-validation (GroupKFold), in which all records associated with a ligand are assigned to the same outer fold. Thus, each held-out ligand is unavailable during fitting.

Inner validation is also ligand-disjoint and is drawn only from the outer-training ligands: 15% of the outer-training ligands of each fold are held out by a grouped shuffle split for early stopping. Feature scaling is fit on the outer-training partition of each fold and then applied to validation and test data. Production cross-validation does not use median imputation; records with missing required fields are quarantined before dataset construction rather than filled. Headline values are the mean and standard deviation (SD) of the five fold-level summaries ($\mathrm{ddof}=1$); they are not pooled-row estimates unless explicitly labeled otherwise. Concatenated held-out rows, including pooled out-of-fold (OOF) diagnostics, are never a substitute for the fold-level headline.

The historical CMI reproduction uses the published official train, test, and external files and is reported as a separate protocol audit. Its test metrics are based on the published five-fold assignment, whereas the released-weight and from-scratch checks use the open files under the protocols described in Supplementary Section S1.5. These values are not used as ligand-disjoint estimates or in workhorse selection. Software versions, checkpoint loading, external-file missing-value handling, and exact train/test-file estimands remain in that Supporting Information subsection.

 2.3 Primary target and mean-of-pairs metrics

The main prediction target is relative selectivity, $\Delta\log K$, for a metal pair. Because the practical decision is pair-specific and pair support is uneven, the primary evaluation unit is the metal pair rather than the individual record, and the headline accuracy metric is a pair-equal mean-of-pairs (MoP) root mean squared error (RMSE).

A pair is *MoP-eligible* in a given fold when at least three held-out ligand rows are available for it. For each MoP-eligible pair $p$, let $\mathrm{RMSE}_p$ be the RMSE calculated over the held-out ligands for that pair. The MoP RMSE is

$$
\mathrm{MoP\ RMSE}
=
\frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{eligible}}|}
\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{eligible}}}
\mathrm{RMSE}_p.
$$

Each eligible pair therefore receives equal weight, regardless of the number of held-out ligands associated with it. By contrast, row-level RMSE weights individual records and consequently gives greater influence to heavily sampled pairs.

The set of MoP-eligible pairs can differ across folds, retention fractions, and risk scores. A model-level all-pair headline is reported only when all five outer folds provide a valid all-pair MoP summary.

Row-level RMSE gives each held-out row equal weight; MoP RMSE gives each supported pair equal weight after pair-level RMSE is calculated. The same distinction applies to the interval diagnostics defined below: row-level prediction interval coverage probability (PICP) and average coverage error (ACE) are row-weighted, whereas MoP PICP and ACE are pair-equal. MoP $R^2$ is the mean of within-pair $R^2$ values. It is reported as an auxiliary quantity only, because within a single species-level pair the observed spread of $\Delta\log K$ is often smaller than the prediction error, and that narrow spread drives within-pair $R^2$ strongly negative even for models whose row-level $R^2$ exceeds 0.7; Supplementary Section S4.2 explains why this quantity should not be read as a goodness-of-fit statistic here.

PICP and ACE are complementary interval diagnostics and are not interchangeable with accuracy metrics. PICP@90 is the empirical fraction of held-out observations that fall inside the nominal 90% predictive interval. ACE is not a single-level quantity: it is the mean absolute deviation between empirical and nominal coverage across the nine nominal levels $\{0.1,0.2,\ldots,0.9\}$,

$$
\mathrm{ACE}=\frac{1}{9}\sum_{q\in\{0.1,\ldots,0.9\}}\bigl|\,\widehat{C}(q)-q\,\bigr|,
$$

where $\widehat{C}(q)$ is empirical coverage of the nominal $q$ interval. A model can therefore have near-nominal PICP@90 and still carry a large ACE if its intervals are miscalibrated away from the 90% level. Row-level PICP and ACE evaluate this over held-out rows; MoP PICP and ACE average the same within-pair quantities over supported pairs.

Retained-subset MoP RMSE is calculated on the pairs that remain supported after score-based row retention. It is therefore an error-ordering estimand, not a claim that the same improvement applies uniformly to every original pair. The pair-acquisition metrics — event precision@$k$, true hit@$k$, and retrospective regret@$k$ — are defined in Methods 2.6.

 2.4 Models and workhorse selection

Point models provide a predicted value $\widehat y$ but no predictive interval; they are an RDKit-descriptor gradient-boosted-tree model (XGBoost,<sup>24</sup> shown as “RDKit XGBoost” in the tables), an RDKit-descriptor multilayer perceptron, and the Chemprop point D-MPNN.<sup>21</sup> Because they carry no uncertainty output, point models are categorically ineligible as downstream workhorses. Direct-UQ models are trained on DS2 and provide both $\widehat y$ and an uncertainty estimate for $\Delta\log K$. The evaluated direct-UQ families include evidential models,<sup>14,15</sup> heteroscedastic models,<sup>18</sup> Monte Carlo (MC) dropout models,<sup>13</sup> full variational Bayesian neural networks (BNNs),<sup>25</sup> spectral-normalized Gaussian-process (SNGP) models,<sup>26</sup> and sparse variational Gaussian-process regression.<sup>16,17</sup> Model-specific interval construction and training settings are in Supplementary Section S2.

The selected workhorse is a five-member heteroscedastic multilayer perceptron (MLP) ensemble.<sup>12,18</sup> Each member uses the shared tabular backbone $[512,512,256]$, input-dependent observation variance, and 30 stochastic dropout passes at prediction. Ensemble, within-member, and input-dependent components are combined using the law of total variance (Supplementary Section S2.4). The resulting $\sigma_{\mathrm{total}}$ is a predictive standard deviation used for empirical risk ordering; it is not an applicability-domain certificate or a direct estimate of laboratory noise. Exact Gaussian process regression (GPR) was computationally infeasible and does not appear in active comparison tables. Dropout is not shared across MLP families. Native Chemprop $\sigma_{\mathrm{total}}$ is a separate model-specific output and is not pooled with the selected-model score. DS1 remains a supporting absolute-binding benchmark in Supplementary Section S3.

**Aggregation convention.** Headline accuracy is pair-equal (MoP) throughout: MoP RMSE is the quantity every downstream result is judged on. Row-weighted values are used where a row-level question is being asked — the interval screen and two of the three selection objectives below, the predicted-magnitude bins of Section 3.4, the score–residual correlations of Table 3, and the support-binned coverage of Section 3.4 — and each is labelled as row-level at the point of use. The two are never compared as though interchangeable, because on this data they can point in opposite directions: Section 3.2 shows that a row-weighted $R^2$ of $0.725$ and a pair-equal $R^2$ of $-3.633$ describe the same model, and that neither is a usable goodness-of-fit statistic.

The *interval screen* admits a model as a workhorse candidate when it has five valid outer-fold summaries, complete native uncertainty output, row-level PICP@90 between 0.85 and 0.95, and row-level ACE no greater than 0.10. These limits are conventions defined for this study, not externally validated calibration thresholds. The screen is row-level by prespecification, and it functions as an admission gate on interval sanity rather than as a ranking. The screen is consequential: the lowest all-pair MoP RMSE among the direct-UQ models belongs to the MC-dropout MLP ensemble ($1.933 \pm 0.093$), but its row-level ACE of $0.114 \pm 0.011$ exceeds the 0.10 limit, so it was screened out before the Pareto step. Point models such as RDKit gradient-boosted trees are excluded a priori for lack of any uncertainty output and are not screened on calibration at all.

Among eligible models, selection uses the prespecified all-pair RMSE–PICP–ACE Pareto procedure. The three objectives, all minimized, are all-pair MoP RMSE, row-level ACE, and $|\mathrm{PICP@90}-0.90|$ computed from the same row-level coverage used by the interval screen. Model A *dominates* model B when it is no worse on all three objectives and strictly better on at least one; the models that no other model dominates form the *Pareto front*. The selected model is the Pareto-front member closest in Euclidean distance to the ideal point after min–max normalization of the three objectives **within the Pareto front**; remaining ties are broken by higher all-pair sign accuracy — the fraction of held-out rows whose predicted $\Delta\log K$ carries the same sign as the observed value — then by higher all-pair MoP $R^2$, then by a prespecified simplicity ranking. Two of the three objectives are therefore row-level and one is pair-equal; the selection was run in that mixed space and is reported as such. The tie-breakers were not reached — the Pareto-front distances separated the candidates outright — so all-pair sign accuracy and MoP $R^2$ did not in fact influence the outcome. MoP PICP and MoP ACE are reported as pair-level diagnostics but enter neither the screen nor the objectives. Figure 1 plots the candidates in pair-equal coordinates for context and is not the space in which the Pareto step was evaluated. Row-level RMSE was not used for selection, and neither retention-curve behavior nor any pair-acquisition metric entered it. Workhorse selection was exploratory and conditional, not a nested statistical test. The screening outcome and selection status of every candidate are in Table S6.1, the fold-wise differences between the selected model and its nearest screen-passing alternatives in Table S6.2, and the fold-local count of pairs contributing to the workhorse MoP summary in Table S6.3. The full evaluated grid across all scopes is Supplementary Section S4, and the direct-UQ screening procedure is Supplementary Section S5.

Chemprop evidential is retained as a scientific comparator because its pair-level accuracy summaries are complete across five folds, but its native DS2 uncertainty output is partial: 13 held-out rows produce invalid native Normal–Inverse-Gamma (NIG) intervals, so its PICP and ACE are conditional on valid intervals and it is not a workhorse candidate. The full variational BNN ensemble has point-prediction results, but its all-pair native interval metrics were not computed for the saved ensemble artifact and are therefore reported as unavailable. Quantities that were not computed, or that are not identifiable under this design, are inventoried in Supplementary Section S11 rather than approximated.

A restricted supplementary diagnostic compares direct DS2 uncertainty with uncertainty propagated from two DS1 absolute-binding predictions. The propagated interval uses the independent-error expression $\sigma_{\Delta}=\sqrt{\sigma_X^2+\sigma_Y^2}$ and is evaluated only on the restricted chemical subset described in Supplementary Section S13. Propagated DS1 intervals are diagnostic differences and are not native DS2 intervals. This diagnostic is not used to select the workhorse and does not replace the all-pair MoP or retention analyses.

Ad hoc DS1 comparators in Table 2 and Supplementary Section S13 are retrained on fold-matched DS1 subsets. For each DS2 outer fold, the DS1 training and validation slices used by that fold’s ad hoc model do not overlap in ligand identity with that fold’s DS2 test partition: every ligand that appears in the DS2 test fold is excluded from DS1 train and val. The two absolute-binding endpoints of each held-out DS2 pair are then predicted with that fold-matched DS1 model, so that the ad hoc comparator faces the same ligand-novelty condition as the direct DS2 models.

 2.5 Distance and risk-ordering analysis

We evaluate candidate scores as empirical predictors of held-out error rather than assuming that any score defines a universal applicability domain. Molecular-distance scores are Tanimoto or descriptor/embedding distances. Support scores measure pair-density risk, defined for a held-out row as $1/(1+n_p)$, where $n_p$ is the number of distinct outer-training ligands carrying the same species-level pair; a row whose metal pair is sparsely represented in training therefore receives a high risk value, and the score is a data-coverage measure that uses no model output. Predictive-uncertainty scores are $\sigma_{\mathrm{total}}$ or one of its components. Empirical risk-ordering analysis is the retention and residual-association evaluation.

The three primary scores are the selected workhorse $\sigma_{\mathrm{total}}$, RDKit feature-centroid Mahalanobis distance, and Tanimoto nearest-neighbor distance. Tanimoto nearest-neighbor (NN) distance is $1-\max$ Tanimoto similarity to the outer-training ligands over 2,048-bit Morgan fingerprints of radius 2.<sup>4</sup> RDKit feature-centroid Mahalanobis distance is the distance to the outer-training centroid under a Ledoit–Wolf shrinkage covariance estimate<sup>27</sup> fit on the fold-local standard-scaled RDKit-plus-metal-plus-medium features. Tanimoto median distance, RDKit feature-median Euclidean distance, $k$-nearest-neighbor ($k$NN) variants, the individual uncertainty components, pair-density risk, and Chemprop embedding distances are registered sensitivity scores in the frozen applicability-domain inventory. In the score names used below and in Table 3, NN denotes a distance to the single closest outer-training row and $k$NN the mean distance to the $k$ closest.

One further sensitivity score, RDKit feature-median Mahalanobis distance, is reported in Figure 2 and Table 3. It is the median distance to *all* outer-training rows in the same Ledoit–Wolf-whitened space as the centroid score, rather than the distance to their centroid. It was computed post hoc from the frozen fold-local feature arrays and held-out fold scores and is not part of the registered applicability-domain inventory; it is therefore a figure-and-table sensitivity comparator rather than a pipeline score. Its provenance is recorded in Supplementary Section S7.

All scores are oriented so that larger values indicate greater predicted risk. Distance scores are fit or calculated using outer-training information only, where the outer-training partition comprises the outer-train and inner-validation rows of that fold. Within each held-out fold, rows are ranked by score, the $\lfloor rn\rfloor$ lowest-risk rows are retained at nominal retention fraction $r$ with ties broken deterministically by record identifier, and MoP RMSE is recomputed on pairs with at least three retained rows. The support rule counts retained rows, not distinct ligands: one ligand can contribute several rows to the same species-level pair through different experimental media. Because different scores retain different supported-pair populations, retained-subset RMSE is interpreted jointly with the number of contributing pairs. Retention-curve area under the curve (AUC) is the trapezoidal integral of retained-subset MoP RMSE over the canonical retention grid $\{0.10,0.20,0.25,0.30,0.40,0.50,0.60,0.70,0.80,0.90,1.00\}$; lower values indicate stronger concentration of low-error predictions at low retention fractions.

The additional fingerprint, descriptor, and Chemprop-embedding scores are registered sensitivity analyses. All candidate scores enter the retention and residual-association evaluation on equal terms; Section 3.4 reports how each performs. Full score formulas and sensitivity inventories are in Supplementary Section S7.

 2.6 Pair acquisition

Pair acquisition is evaluated as a retrospective held-out ranking simulation with prospective information structure. Rankings use only model predictions and uncertainty generated before held-out labels are used for scoring. However, held-out event counts are used to define eligible pair-tasks, and held-out labels are necessarily used to calculate precision, hit, and regret. The experiment therefore does not constitute prospective experimental validation.

A species-level pair is a metal-species relationship in DS2, preserving the element and oxidation state on both sides of the comparison. A pair-task is a species-level pair combined with an orientation and a specified acquisition objective. A high-selectivity pair can therefore contribute separate canonical and reversed pair-tasks, whereas the near-zero objective is orientation-invariant. A pair-task is *acquisition-eligible* only when it contains more than one held-out event for the relevant objective; this is a different rule from MoP eligibility in Section 2.3 and the two are never combined.

High-selectivity: target-direction $\Delta\log K>1$. Near-zero: $|\Delta\log K|<1$. Eligibility: $n_{\mathrm{event}}>1$. A pair-task contributes to Precision@$k$, Hit@$k$, and retrospective regret@$k$ only when it has at least $k$ held-out rows.

Ligands are ranked using only $\widehat y$ and $\sigma_{\mathrm{total}}$. Distance scores are not ranking inputs.

For an oriented high-selectivity task, define the oriented target and prediction as

$$
y_i^\star =
\begin{cases}
y_i, & \text{canonical direction},\\
-y_i, & \text{reversed direction},
\end{cases}
\qquad
\widehat y_i^\star =
\begin{cases}
\widehat y_i, & \text{canonical direction},\\
-\widehat y_i, & \text{reversed direction}.
\end{cases}
$$

The uncertainty is unchanged by reversing the target orientation, so $\sigma_{\mathrm{total},i}^\star=\sigma_{\mathrm{total},i}$.

For high selectivity, the ranking scores are oriented predicted selectivity, oriented lower confidence bounds (LCB), and the standardized threshold-margin score:

$$
s_{\mathrm{value},i}=\widehat y_i^\star,
\qquad
s_{\mathrm{LCB},z,i}=\widehat y_i^\star-z\sigma_{\mathrm{total},i},
\qquad
s_{\mathrm{margin},i}=
\frac{\widehat y_i^\star-1}{\sigma_{\mathrm{total},i}},
\quad z\in\{1,2,3\}.
$$

Higher values indicate a more favorable candidate for the high-selectivity objective.

For near-zero selection, candidates are ranked from low to high predicted magnitude. The predicted-magnitude score is $-|\widehat y_i|$, the uncertainty-aware LCB variants are $-(|\widehat y_i|+z\sigma_{\mathrm{total},i})$, and the standardized margin is $(1-|\widehat y_i|)/\sigma_{\mathrm{total},i}$. Higher values indicate a more favorable candidate for the near-zero objective.

The LCB-$z$ rules are uncertainty-penalized ranking scores with $z\in\{1,2,3\}$. The threshold-margin score is a standardized distance beyond the event threshold. It is not the Gaussian expected-improvement integral; that alternative was not computed.

Two of the three reported metrics are the standard shortlist quantities: event precision@$k$ is the fraction of the top-$k$ shortlist that are observed events, and true hit@$k$ is the indicator that the shortlist contains at least one event. They coincide at $k=1$, and both treat every observed event equally, so a shortlist that captures a weakly qualifying ligand scores as well as one that captures the strongest available ligand. Their formal definitions are given in Supplementary Section S10.1.

Retrospective regret@$k$ is scored on the same within-pair rankings but measures the leftover objective gap rather than event presence, and is therefore an optimization diagnostic rather than an event metric. Let $\mathcal{P}$ be the held-out ligand pool of one pair-task, and let $B_k\subseteq\mathcal{P}$ be the top $k$ ranked ligands in that pair-task. Ranking is never pooled across pairs within a fold. For high selectivity, $y^{\star}$ is the target-direction observed selectivity (canonical $y$, reversed $-y$), and

$$
R_k^{\mathrm{high}}
=
\max_{i\in\mathcal{P}} y^{\star}_i
-
\max_{i\in B_k} y^{\star}_i.
$$

For near-zero selectivity,

$$
R_k^{\mathrm{near}}
=
\min_{i\in B_k}|y_i|
-
\min_{i\in\mathcal{P}}|y_i|.
$$

Lower values are better. The units are $\Delta\log K$. $R_k$ is defined only when $|\mathcal{P}|\ge k$, the same support rule used for Precision@$k$ and Hit@$k$. Regret is nonnegative. Zero means that $B_k$ contains a pool-optimal ligand, including tied optima. Because $\mathcal{P}$ is the set of held-out rows carrying a finite ranking score, the pool optimum is independent of the ranking rule whenever $\widehat y$ and $\sigma_{\mathrm{total}}$ are finite on every held-out row; this holds for the selected workhorse, which has complete native uncertainty output on all five folds. Rankings, tie-breaking, and shortlists are unchanged from the hit@$k$ and precision@$k$ calculations; held-out labels identify the pool optimum for scoring only.

The six rules compared are predicted selectivity, LCB-1, LCB-2, LCB-3, threshold margin, and random. Random ranking uses 20 within-pair permutations (seed 0). Metrics, including retrospective regret@$k$, are aggregated by pair-equal mean within each fold, then fold mean ± SD. The complete $k=1,5,10$ inventories are Tables S10.2 (true hit and event precision) and S10.3 (retrospective regret), with the eligible pair-task counts in Table S10.1; Table 4 and Figure 4 report the main-text subset.

 3. Results and Discussion

 3.1 Validation regime defines the generalization claim

The first result concerns not model architecture, but the interpretation of model performance. The official CMI assignment and the ligand-disjoint evaluation probe different forms of generalization and should therefore not be treated as interchangeable estimates of model quality.

The official CMI test assignment contains substantial ligand reuse between the training and test partitions. Of the 1,670 test ligands, 1,568 also occur in the training data, corresponding to 93.9% overlap; 97.5% of official test rows involve a ligand that is present in training. Table 1 sets that overlap alongside the accuracy obtained under each regime, so that the three rows can be read as three different questions rather than three attempts at the same one. That overlap does not invalidate the original benchmark, but it does mean the published test performance is measured partly in a familiar-ligand regime. The model can encounter the same ligand during training even when the associated metal or experimental context differs. Such a split is relevant to interpolation across chemical contexts, but it does not directly estimate performance for a ligand that is unavailable during model fitting.

The reduction in performance relative to the official test assignment does not indicate a failure of the model or of the original benchmark. It demonstrates that excluding ligand reuse changes the estimand from prediction in a partially familiar chemical neighborhood to prediction for ligands unavailable during model fitting. The difference between the two regimes therefore quantifies how strongly apparent performance depends on the type of generalization being tested.

Neither regime is a universal applicability-domain test, because a test record can be novel with respect to its ligand while remaining familiar with respect to the metal pair, oxidation state, or experimental conditions, and the converse is equally possible. The ligand-disjoint regime is used for the main model comparisons because it is the most direct test of the intended deployment scenario: prioritizing ligands that were not available during model fitting. The next step is therefore to establish how different DS1 models perform under this regime before asking whether absolute-binding predictions can support the pairwise selectivity task.

 3.2 From absolute binding to pairwise selectivity

The ligand-disjoint DS1 benchmark establishes the point-model baseline for absolute stability-constant prediction. DS1 retains the reported $\log K$ value for each normalized metal–ligand measurement and is evaluated on metal-complex records. Among the point models, the RDKit MLP gives the lowest RMSE, $2.211 \pm 0.183$, with an $R^2$ of $0.834 \pm 0.018$. RDKit XGBoost gives an RMSE of $2.292 \pm 0.161$ and an $R^2$ of $0.821 \pm 0.020$, while Chemprop point gives an RMSE of $2.330 \pm 0.265$ and an $R^2$ of $0.816 \pm 0.027$.

In this ligand-disjoint setting, the RDKit models therefore provide a strong and substantially simpler baseline. Their performance is comparable to, or better than, the Chemprop model, while the graph model costs between about thirty-five and a hundred and thirty times more per 1,000 training rows (Table 2). This comparison is especially relevant for the present study because the subsequent analyses concern an entire prediction-and-prioritization pipeline rather than a single architecture. When a more complex representation does not produce a consistent improvement in the target estimand, the lower-cost RDKit representation is a practical default and provides a more demanding baseline for assessing whether uncertainty estimation adds value. The model carried through the rest of this work is accordingly an RDKit-descriptor ensemble.

One difficulty is invisible in any of these accuracy columns, because part of the error lies in the target itself. Among 3,048 nominally matching metal-complex measurement groups in DS1 — same ligand, metal, oxidation state, temperature, ionic strength and electrolyte — 1,234 disagree by more than $0.20$ in $\log K$ and 368 by more than $1$, with the largest spans exceeding seven log units. Records are retained exactly as received rather than adjudicated, so a model trained here must learn through that disagreement, and prediction error is never purely model error (Supplementary Section S1). That is a further reason to ask for a per-prediction risk estimate rather than an average accuracy: some regions of this literature are simply less reproducible than others.

Absolute binding is not the final prediction task. For metal extraction the relevant decision is comparative — whether a ligand favors one metal species over a competing one — and the obvious first attempt is to subtract two DS1 predictions for the same ligand. That construction is chemically intuitive and gives the DS1 models a fair baseline, but it also shows why absolute-binding accuracy cannot stand in for selectivity accuracy: errors in the two predictions may cancel, accumulate, or vary systematically with the metal pair and chemical context. The ad hoc estimate must therefore be judged at the pair level.

Table 2 also shows why this work does not use $R^2$ as a headline in either aggregation, and the reason is worth stating because the two $R^2$ columns look like a contradiction. For the selected workhorse, row-level $R^2$ is $0.725 \pm 0.031$ while MoP $R^2$ is $-3.633 \pm 1.993$; every model in the grid shows the same reversal.

Neither number means what it appears to. Pooled across held-out rows, the variance of $\Delta\log K$ is dominated by *which* metal pair a row belongs to: a Cu²⁺/Zn²⁺ comparison sits in a different part of the range from a Ca²⁺/Mg²⁺ one. Decomposing the held-out target, $0.685 \pm 0.033$ of its total variance is between-pair. A predictor that returns the mean $\Delta\log K$ of the relevant metal pair and uses no ligand information at all therefore scores a row-level $R^2$ of $0.685 \pm 0.033$ — against the workhorse's $0.725 \pm 0.031$. The model's entire ligand-level contribution, which is the whole object of the exercise, is $0.039 \pm 0.009$ of pooled variance. A row-weighted $R^2$ near $0.73$ is mostly the reward for knowing a metal pair the experimentalist has already chosen.

MoP $R^2$ removes that free variance by normalizing within each pair, but it is unstable in the other direction. Its fold means are strongly negative, yet the median *within-pair* $R^2$ is positive at $0.149 \pm 0.133$ and $0.596 \pm 0.075$ of supported pairs have a positive value. The negative average is produced by a tail of pairs whose held-out $\Delta\log K$ barely varies, where the normalizing denominator is near zero and the ratio diverges. So MoP $R^2$ does not show that the model is generally worse than a pair mean; it shows that $R^2$ is the wrong summary for narrow-spread groups, exactly as Methods 2.3 and Supplementary Section S4.2 state. The full decomposition is Table S4.5.

The practical conclusion is that both $R^2$ columns are auxiliary and neither is a goodness-of-fit statistic here. The quantity that survives both objections is MoP RMSE, which is in the units of the target and has no denominator to destabilize, and it is the headline this paper reports. On that measure no model here — ad hoc or direct — predicts pairwise selectivity accurately enough to be applied indiscriminately. All-pair MoP RMSE stays near 2 $\Delta\log K$ units against a selectivity threshold of 1 $\Delta\log K$ unit. A model of that accuracy cannot responsibly be used to make a prediction for every candidate and act on all of them. It can still support a campaign, but only if it also indicates *which* of its predictions to act on — that is, only if it carries a per-prediction risk estimate. Uncertainty quantification is therefore not an optional refinement here; it is the property that makes the model usable at all. And because that risk estimate will be used to decide what to test, it has to be trustworthy across the range of confidence levels, not merely present.

The ad hoc route fails that requirement. Uncertainty-aware DS1 models can be used as ad hoc selectivity predictors by propagating their absolute-binding uncertainties to the difference between two metal predictions under the independent-error approximation. Their point-level accuracy is competitive — Chemprop evidential ad hoc gives an all-pair MoP RMSE of $2.172 \pm 0.195$, within the spread of the direct models — but the propagated intervals are badly calibrated, with MoP PICP@90 of $0.963 \pm 0.043$ and an ACE of $0.378 \pm 0.066$. Intervals that wide flag almost nothing as risky and so cannot separate reliable predictions from unreliable ones. Every direct DS2 model with a computed interval is better calibrated than this on the same measure, and the best of them by a wide margin: the selected heteroscedastic ensemble reaches a MoP ACE of $0.153 \pm 0.009$ at comparable accuracy, less than half the ad hoc value, and even the weakest direct model (Chemprop evidential, $0.240 \pm 0.015$) improves on it substantially.

The failure is informative rather than incidental. Uncertainty learned for absolute $\log K$ does not transfer automatically to a difference between two predictions, because the target, the error structure, and the dependence between the two predictions all change, and the propagation step discards the covariance term that would account for the last of these. Training on the pairwise target instead attaches the uncertainty to the quantity that the experimental decision actually consumes. Direct DS2 modeling is adopted for the downstream analysis on that basis: comparable accuracy, substantially better calibration, and a risk estimate defined on the right quantity.

Among the direct-UQ models, the heteroscedastic MLP ensemble was selected as the workhorse for the downstream analysis. Nearby models perform similarly, including the evidential MLP ensemble with MoP RMSE of $2.009 \pm 0.112$ and the MC-dropout MLP with $1.999 \pm 0.104$.  3.4 Empirical applicability domain and prediction-risk ordering

A distance from the training set describes chemical similarity; it does not by itself say whether a prediction will be accurate. We therefore treat every candidate risk score as an empirical claim to be tested: a score earns standing only if the predictions it judges safe are in fact more accurate under this validation design. The workhorse uncertainty is compared on those terms against fingerprint distances, RDKit descriptor-space distances, pair-support measures, individual uncertainty components, and learned-representation distances from the Chemprop evidential model, with the complete sensitivity inventory in Supplementary Section S7.

The primary comparison is fixed-retention. Within each held-out fold, predictions are ranked by risk, the lowest-risk fraction is retained, and MoP RMSE is recomputed on that subset — a direct test of whether a score concentrates low-error predictions at the top of its ranking, with no arbitrary distance threshold imposed.

At 50% nominal retention, ranking by $\sigma_{\mathrm{total}}$ reduces all-pair MoP RMSE from the unfiltered value of $2.078 \pm 0.130$ to $0.730 \pm 0.084$. The corresponding retained-subset MoP RMSE is $1.586 \pm 0.198$ for RDKit feature-centroid Mahalanobis distance, $1.580 \pm 0.193$ for RDKit feature-median Mahalanobis distance, $1.697 \pm 0.235$ for Tanimoto median distance, and $1.891 \pm 0.129$ for Tanimoto nearest-neighbor distance. Among the principal scores, model-derived uncertainty therefore concentrates low-error predictions substantially more effectively than either structural or descriptor-space distance.

Fixed-retention performance evaluates score behavior at the subset level, whereas score–residual correlation evaluates the same relationship at the individual-prediction level. We therefore calculated fold-local Spearman correlations between each risk score and the absolute held-out residual. Larger positive values indicate that higher scores are associated with larger prediction errors.

The correlation analysis provides a row-level counterpart to the retention results, and it is more damaging to the distance scores than the retention curves alone suggest. The workhorse $\sigma_{\mathrm{total}}$ shows a strong positive association with absolute residual ($0.648 \pm 0.020$). The descriptor-space distances are positive but weak, the strongest of them reaching only $0.250 \pm 0.078$. The remaining scores do not order error in the expected direction at all. Ten of the twenty-three scores in Table 3 have negative fold-mean rank correlations with the absolute residual, and they are not a random subset: they comprise all seven Chemprop learned-representation distances and three of the four fingerprint distances, including Tanimoto nearest-neighbor distance at $-0.087 \pm 0.070$ and the Chemprop embedding Mahalanobis nearest-neighbor score at $-0.120 \pm 0.059$.

The pattern of the signs matters more than their size. The individual correlations are small — the largest in magnitude is $-0.120 \pm 0.059$ — and none is large enough that inverting it would be worth doing. What is hard to dismiss is that the sign is negative in *every* nearest-neighbor and embedding score across two unrelated representations — seven Chemprop and three Tanimoto — rather than scattering either side of zero as it would if these distances simply carried no signal. Supplementary Figure S19 plots all twenty-three scores of Table 3 on one axis, where that division is easier to see than to assemble from the table. The one fingerprint score that is positive, Tanimoto median distance at $+0.102 \pm 0.096$, is a median over all training ligands rather than a nearest-neighbor distance. Every other positive distance belongs to the joint RDKit descriptor space, which encodes metal and medium alongside the ligand; the two positive scores that are not distances at all — pair-density risk and the uncertainty components — are treated separately below.

The honest reading is therefore not that these scores are anti-correlated with error, which the fold SDs will not support, but that they carry no usable risk information and may carry a slight adverse tilt. Either way an applicability domain drawn as "reject the predictions furthest from the training set in fingerprint or embedding space" would, on this task, discard predictions no more error-prone than the ones it keeps: the ten-percent error-enrichment ratio for Tanimoto nearest-neighbor distance is $0.768 \pm 0.190$, and one lies within a standard deviation of that. Such a filter is not actively harmful to accuracy so much as pure cost — it removes candidates from consideration and returns nothing measurable for them (Table S7.3). A plausible reading is that in a structure-plus-context task the ligands sitting far from the training set in ligand-only space are frequently simple, well-characterized chelators measured under common conditions, while the hard cases are ordinary ligands in sparsely sampled metal and medium contexts that ligand-space distance cannot see. Whatever the mechanism, the practical conclusion is that these distances are not weak predictors of error so much as non-predictors of it.

The correlation and retention analyses therefore agree. Model-derived uncertainty identifies the predictions most likely to be inaccurate. The descriptor-space distances do so weakly but measurably, reducing retained-subset error by about a quarter at half retention, and Tanimoto median distance by about a fifth. The nearest-neighbor and embedding variants do not do so at all.

The same ordering is observed across the complete retention curves. Retention-curve AUC is $0.835 \pm 0.061$ for $\sigma_{\mathrm{total}}$, compared with $1.441 \pm 0.116$ for RDKit feature-centroid Mahalanobis distance and $1.719 \pm 0.102$ for Tanimoto nearest-neighbor distance. Tanimoto median distance gives $1.500 \pm 0.141$. RDKit feature-median Mahalanobis distance gives $1.439 \pm 0.116$ and tracks the centroid score closely. Lower AUC indicates more effective concentration of low-error predictions at low retention fractions.

The most informative comparison in this section is with a score that uses no chemistry at all. Pair-density risk — the inverse training support of the row's metal pair, which reads neither the molecular structure nor any model output — is weaker than the workhorse uncertainty ($0.956 \pm 0.176$ at 50% retention against $0.730 \pm 0.084$) but stronger there than every molecular-distance score tested, the closest of which are the two descriptor-space Mahalanobis variants at $1.580 \pm 0.193$ and $1.586 \pm 0.198$ (Table S7.4). That is not a single lucky operating point: pair-density risk gives a lower retained-subset MoP RMSE than *every* distance in the inventory at all seven retention fractions from 30% to 90% (Figure 2A and Supplementary Figure S10). Simply counting how many training ligands a metal pair carries therefore orders held-out error better than measuring how far a ligand sits from the training set, across most of the usable range of the grid. The advantage does reverse in the extreme tail: at 10% retention the counting rule falls below the random-equivalent enrichment, at $0.949 \pm 0.094$ against $1.602 \pm 0.338$ for the descriptor-space distance, and it is the only one of the four scores in Figure 2 to do so (Table S7.3, where twelve of the twenty-two registered scores also fall below one). A coverage count is the better guide to typical risk and the worse guide to the tail, plausibly because $1/(1+n_p)$ is coarse and heavily tied, so a very low retention cut falls inside a large block of equally-supported rows that the deterministic tie-break then splits arbitrarily. What makes the comparison interesting is what it implies about the axis being measured. How thinly a metal pair is sampled is a large and monotone axis of difficulty here: across the pair-support bins of Table S8.3, held-out row-level RMSE falls from $5.045 \pm 0.997$ on pairs carrying one to five training ligands to $1.482 \pm 0.079$ on pairs carrying more than fifty. It is also an axis no ligand-space geometry can see. The workhorse sees it without being shown it — mean $\sigma_{\mathrm{total}}$ falls in step, from $3.923 \pm 1.416$ to $1.293 \pm 0.256$, although pair support is never a feature and never enters the loss — and coverage moves with the same variable in the direction that matters for a user, with row-level PICP@90 declining from $0.909 \pm 0.018$ in the best-supported bin to $0.803 \pm 0.036$ where the metal pair has no training ligands at all. Section 3.6 returns to that undercoverage as a limitation; Supplementary Section S8 gives the full binning and explains why this axis cannot be ranked against ligand novelty, which the design holds constant.

The retained-subset results should be interpreted together with the pair-support panel. At 50% retention, $\sigma_{\mathrm{total}}$ retains $328.2 \pm 19.9$ MoP-eligible pairs, compared with $549.8 \pm 43.8$ for the RDKit centroid Mahalanobis score, $548.4 \pm 43.7$ for the RDKit median Mahalanobis score, $562.8 \pm 45.4$ for Tanimoto median distance, and $601.4 \pm 52.5$ for Tanimoto nearest-neighbor distance. The retained predictions are therefore not evaluated on a common pair population. This is an inherent feature of evaluating a score as a risk-ordering rule: a strong score may preferentially retain predictions from pair contexts in which the model is better supported and more accurate. It also raises the obvious objection to the headline comparison — that $\sigma_{\mathrm{total}}$ looks better partly because it is scored on an easier set of pairs.

That objection can be tested directly, by restricting every score to the pairs that *all* of them keep at 50% retention, a common population of $298 \pm 16$ pairs per fold. On that matched set the distance scores improve substantially, confirming that part of their apparent weakness was the harder pair population they retained, while the workhorse uncertainty barely moves, from $0.730 \pm 0.084$ to $0.720 \pm 0.098$, because it was already being scored on the pairs it retains. The gap therefore narrows — a 65% reduction against the unfiltered $2.078$ for $\sigma_{\mathrm{total}}$, against 50% for the best distance — but it does not close and it does not change direction. The raw comparison overstates how weak the distance scores are; the matched comparison is the one to quote, and the conclusion survives it. Reported values throughout this section are the free-population ones unless stated; the matched comparison is Table S7.5, computed on $\sigma_{\mathrm{total}}$, both Mahalanobis aggregations, and both Tanimoto aggregations; the pair inventory these populations are drawn from is described in Table S8.5 and Supplementary Figure S12.

Uncertainty also provides information beyond predicted selectivity magnitude. Across held-out rows, the rank correlation between $\sigma_{\mathrm{total}}$ and absolute residual is $0.648 \pm 0.020$, compared with $0.541 \pm 0.029$ for $|\widehat{y}|$. Because uncertainty increases with predicted magnitude, this comparison alone does not establish an additional risk signal. However, after controlling for $|\widehat{y}|$ using a fold-local partial rank correlation, the association between $\sigma_{\mathrm{total}}$ and absolute residual remains positive at $0.428 \pm 0.026$. The uncertainty score therefore carries risk-ordering information beyond predicted magnitude, without any claim of causal independence or of a unique physical source of error; as stated in Methods 2.1.4, $\sigma_{\mathrm{total}}$ is a composite signal whose practical value is that its ranking tracks held-out error.

Within that composite the three variance components do not contribute equally. The input-dependent (aleatoric) term alone reaches a Spearman correlation of $0.646 \pm 0.020$ with the absolute residual, reproducing the full $\sigma_{\mathrm{total}}$ to within $0.002$, while the within-member and between-member ensemble-spread terms give $0.635 \pm 0.025$ and $0.589 \pm 0.019$; in every fold the input-dependent term also accounts for the overwhelming majority of row-level predictive variance (Supplementary Figure S8). The ensemble costs roughly five times a single heteroscedastic head (Table 2) and buys coverage at the operating level rather than ranking power, so a practitioner who needs only to rank candidates by risk can use one head at a fifth of the price. Supplementary Section S2.4 gives that comparison.

Magnitude-based retention provides a complementary comparison. Ranking by $|\widehat{y}|$ alone reduces retained-subset error because predictions with larger magnitude are more difficult on average. At 50% retention, however, magnitude-based ranking gives an all-pair MoP RMSE of $1.088 \pm 0.061$, compared with $0.730 \pm 0.084$ for $\sigma_{\mathrm{total}}$. The lower error achieved by uncertainty ranking, together with the positive magnitude-adjusted association, shows that $\sigma_{\mathrm{total}}$ is not merely a relabeling of high predicted selectivity.

Figure 3 shows the structure behind this comparison. Held-out error and predicted uncertainty both rise steeply and in parallel with predicted selectivity magnitude: row-level RMSE increases from $0.906 \pm 0.071$ in the $|\widehat y|<0.5$ bin to $5.112 \pm 0.343$ above $|\widehat y|=8$, while mean $\sigma_{\mathrm{total}}$ increases from $0.676 \pm 0.080$ to $5.224 \pm 0.653$ across the same bins. This coupling is what makes the magnitude-adjusted analysis necessary, and it is consistent with the objective-dependent ranking behavior described in Section 3.5: the region where the model is least reliable is the region a high-selectivity campaign most wants to explore. The association is descriptive; no causal decomposition of the error into magnitude-driven and uncertainty-driven components is claimed.

Taken together, the retention and correlation analyses say that an applicability domain has to be earned against held-out outcomes rather than assumed from geometry. None of the tested molecular distances orders held-out error strongly enough to act on — no fold-mean Spearman $\rho$ exceeds $0.25$, and most of the fingerprint and embedding scores are negative — so a distance threshold applied here would describe chemical proximity without establishing prediction reliability. The workhorse uncertainty shows both a strong score–residual association and a substantial reduction in retained-subset MoP RMSE, and is therefore the best validated risk-ordering variable available for this evaluation, tied only with its own input-dependent component. It is the score carried into the pair-specific prioritization of the next section. The claim remains relative to this target, representation, data distribution, validation regime, and error criterion.

 3.5 Pair-specific ligand prioritization

 3.5.1 The ranking benchmark

Bulk regression metrics compare models; they do not represent how a selectivity model is used. In a campaign the model ranks candidate ligands for one metal pair and a few are tested. We therefore evaluate the six ranking rules of Methods 2.6 — predicted selectivity, LCB-1, LCB-2, LCB-3, threshold margin, and random — within each held-out species-level pair, on two objectives that a real campaign distinguishes: recovering ligands beyond a selectivity threshold, and finding ligands with little preference between the two metals.

The three metrics answer different questions about the same shortlist. Event precision and true hit rate describe event recovery; retrospective regret describes optimization quality. A ranking can therefore achieve a high hit rate while still missing the strongest available candidate, and the results below show exactly that dissociation.

 3.5.2 High-selectivity ranking

Threshold margin gives the highest high-selectivity event recovery of the tested strategies, on every event metric and at every shortlist size. At $k=1$ its event precision is $0.831 \pm 0.059$ against $0.639 \pm 0.035$ for random ranking; at $k=5$ its true-hit rate is $0.962 \pm 0.021$ against $0.852 \pm 0.021$; at $k=10$ it reaches $0.977 \pm 0.011$. Its margin over ranking by predicted selectivity alone is small — $0.823 \pm 0.067$ at $k=1$ — and single comparisons are inside one fold SD, so the two should not be called separated on any one number. What is consistent is the direction: threshold margin is ahead of the value-only rule on all four high-selectivity event metrics, and the gap is widest where a campaign has the least room, at $k=10$ ($0.977 \pm 0.011$ against $0.953 \pm 0.017$). The lower-confidence-bound rules move in the opposite direction, and steeply. Event precision@1 falls from $0.795 \pm 0.049$ at LCB-1 to $0.732 \pm 0.047$ at LCB-2 and $0.669 \pm 0.050$ at LCB-3 (Table S10.2). Every one of those is below the value-only baseline: subtracting uncertainty from a high-selectivity score does not merely add nothing, it removes value the model already had. At the strongest penalty it goes further and crosses the random line — at $k=5$ LCB-3 recovers an event in $0.813 \pm 0.016$ of pair-tasks against $0.852 \pm 0.021$ for random ranking, and at $k=10$ in $0.821 \pm 0.016$ against $0.893 \pm 0.027$. An aggressively uncertainty-penalized shortlist is a worse way to find a high-selectivity ligand than drawing candidates at random.

The two rules use the same $\sigma_{\mathrm{total}}$ and reach opposite outcomes, so the difference lies in how it is combined with the prediction. LCB subtracts $z\sigma_{\mathrm{total}}$ from the predicted value, which is a penalty in the units of the target: because $\sigma_{\mathrm{total}}$ grows with $|\widehat y|$ (Figure 3A), the subtraction is largest exactly where the predicted selectivity is largest, and the rule demotes the candidates the objective was defined to find. Threshold margin instead divides the distance past the event threshold by $\sigma_{\mathrm{total}}$, which is dimensionless and asks a different question — how confidently does this candidate clear the bar — so a large uncertainty attached to a large predicted selectivity is discounted rather than disqualifying.

The wider lesson is that a good risk-ordering score is not automatically a good acquisition rule. $\sigma_{\mathrm{total}}$ reliably flags the predictions carrying greater expected error (Section 3.4), but subtracting that flag from a high-selectivity score conflates two objectives that pull in opposite directions exactly where uncertainty and predicted magnitude are coupled: minimizing expected prediction risk, and discovering the strongest candidate. How the score is combined with the prediction decides which of the two it serves.

 3.5.3 Near-zero ranking

On the near-zero objective every uncertainty-aware rule helps, and they help by almost the same amount. Event precision@1 rises from $0.748 \pm 0.020$ for the value-only rule to $0.811 \pm 0.029$ (LCB-2), $0.806$ (LCB-1), $0.802$ (LCB-3) and $0.799$ (threshold margin), against $0.569 \pm 0.027$ for random ranking — a spread of $0.012$ across the four uncertainty-aware rules against fold SDs near $0.03$ (Table S10.2). At $k=5$ threshold margin is nominally the best of them ($0.704 \pm 0.015$), as it is on true hit@5 ($0.980 \pm 0.007$). The improvement is therefore a property of using uncertainty on a near-zero target at all, not of a tuned penalty or a particular functional form.

Taken with the high-selectivity result, this is the practical finding of the section. Threshold margin is ahead of or level with the value-only baseline on eleven of the twelve event metrics across both objectives, and the twelfth is a $0.001$ deficit on near-zero true hit@10 ($0.994 \pm 0.005$ against $0.995 \pm 0.004$) that no reading of the fold SDs would call a difference. LCB, by contrast, buys a near-zero gain that threshold margin also delivers and pays for it with a high-selectivity collapse. If a single rule has to be chosen before the objective is known — which is the ordinary situation when a model is handed to an experimental group — threshold margin is the one that cannot go badly wrong.

Regret is the one axis on which the ranking changes, and it is worth separating from event recovery because it answers a different question: not whether the shortlist contains a qualifying ligand, but how close its best member comes to the best candidate available. On the near-zero objective every uncertainty-aware rule beats the value-only one at $k=1$ — LCB-1 at $0.515 \pm 0.038$ and threshold margin at $0.583 \pm 0.051$ against $0.708 \pm 0.094$, with random at $1.585 \pm 0.144$ — and by $k=5$ they are indistinguishable. On the high-selectivity objective the order reverses: ranking by predicted selectivity gives the smallest gap to the strongest available ligand ($1.738 \pm 0.376$ at $k=1$, $0.540 \pm 0.152$ at $k=5$), ahead of threshold margin ($2.508 \pm 0.244$ and $0.754 \pm 0.198$), because a rule that rewards confident threshold crossing will prefer a safely qualifying ligand over a spectacular but uncertain one. A campaign that wants any qualifying ligand should rank by threshold margin; one that wants the single best ligand it can find should rank by predicted selectivity and accept a lower hit rate.

The asymmetry follows from the error structure of the model. Predictions near zero have lower typical error than large-magnitude ones — row-level RMSE is $1.074 \pm 0.056$ for $|\widehat y|<1$ against $2.807 \pm 0.261$ for $|\widehat y|\ge1$ (Table S10.4) — so a candidate predicted near zero but assigned high uncertainty is genuinely at risk of leaving the near-zero window, and penalizing uncertainty removes it. The same penalty is counterproductive when the target region is the one where uncertainty is highest by construction. A corollary is that true-hit rates converge across all non-random rules once the shortlist is long enough, leaving event precision and regret as the metrics that still separate them.

 3.5.4 Threshold sensitivity

The supporting threshold-sensitivity analysis preserves the main pattern when the high-selectivity criterion is made more stringent ($\Delta\log K>2$) and the near-zero criterion narrower ($|\Delta\log K|<1/2$). Threshold margin again leads on high-selectivity event recovery, by a margin no larger than at the primary thresholds (true hit@5 of $0.967 \pm 0.026$ against $0.833 \pm 0.022$ for random ranking), while predicted selectivity retains an advantage in approaching the strongest candidate (regret@1 of $1.813 \pm 0.394$ against $2.534 \pm 0.396$). For the stricter near-zero objective, uncertainty-aware ranking again improves event recovery and shortlist quality relative to random selection. Table S10.6 gives the compact $k=1,5$ subset of this sensitivity analysis and Table S10.7 the eligible pair counts under the stricter thresholds; the complete $k=1,5,10$ inventory is plotted in Supplementary Figure S14.

The result is therefore not an artifact of the primary event thresholds, and the ordering it implies is stable: threshold margin for event recovery, value-only ranking for optimization toward the strongest candidate, and uncertainty penalties only where the target lies in a lower-error regime.

The pair-specific ranking results complete the link between empirical risk ordering and experimental prioritization. The workhorse uncertainty is not uniformly beneficial when inserted into every acquisition rule; its value depends on how it interacts with the target region, the error distribution, and the objective that defines a successful experiment. Two supporting diagnostics reinforce this reading without extending it. On a restricted rare-earth-element pair subset, uncertainty-aware ad hoc DS1 models did not consistently outperform the direct DS2 workhorse, and their propagated intervals were markedly overconservative, which supports attaching uncertainty to $\Delta\log K$ directly rather than reconstructing it from two absolute-binding intervals (Supplementary Section S13). Separately, most nominal 90% intervals overlap zero — $0.742 \pm 0.024$ of held-out rows — so the intervals order risk without certifying the sign of the predicted selectivity (Supplementary Section S9). Both are restricted or descriptive diagnostics and neither reopens the all-pair evaluation. The remaining limitations of the study are set out next.

 3.6 Limitations and scope

The main limitation is the uneven chemical coverage of the dataset. Ligand-disjoint validation evaluates prediction for unseen ligands, but it does not test extrapolation to entirely unseen metal pairs, oxidation states, or experimental media. Performance in sparsely represented pair contexts is measurably worse than the all-pair averages suggest, and so is calibration: as reported in Section 3.4, nominal 90% intervals undercover by roughly ten points where the metal pair has no training ligands (Table S8.3 and Supplementary Figure S11). Because $\sigma_{\mathrm{total}}$ still rises with sparsity, those predictions are ranked as risky and are the first a retention rule discards; but an individual interval width on a thinly supported metal pair should be read as a lower bound, and pair support is worth reporting alongside any single prediction.

The rare-earth-element analysis should be interpreted particularly cautiously. Although the dataset contains a restricted rare-earth-element (REE) subset of REE–REE pairs, it represents only a fraction of the full DS2 pair space, and its pair composition and support differ from the all-pair benchmark. The restricted analysis was not designed to establish general performance for rare-earth extraction, and no dedicated combined REE–high-selectivity validation was performed.

The literature measurements also contain disagreement between nominally matching records. This variability reflects the heterogeneous experimental sources and limits the extent to which prediction error can be separated into model error and experimental noise. This is the reason $\sigma_{\mathrm{total}}$ is treated throughout as a composite prediction-risk signal rather than as an estimate of laboratory or mechanistic uncertainty.

Finally, the applicability-domain and acquisition results are conditional on the present DS2 representation, ligand-disjoint split, and tested risk scores. The ranking benchmark is retrospective: held-out predictions are ranked before their labels are scored, but task eligibility and regret evaluation use held-out outcomes. It therefore evaluates prospective-style shortlist quality rather than prospective experimental performance. A dedicated metal-pair holdout and prospective experimental campaign would be required to establish generalization beyond the present validation regime.

 4. Conclusions

This study shows that the usefulness of a cheminformatics model for metal–ligand selectivity depends less on its bulk regression score than on whether its validation and uncertainty estimates reflect the decision the model is intended to support. For heterogeneous coordination-chemistry data, a benchmark split should therefore be interpreted as a definition of chemical novelty, not as a generic measure of external validity.

The result with the widest reach is that an applicability domain should not be assumed to be a geometric region in molecular space. A distance from the training set earns standing as a risk measure only when it is shown to track held-out error, and on this task most of the standard choices do not earn it. Every fingerprint nearest-neighbor variant and every learned-embedding distance we tested correlates with the absolute residual in the *wrong* direction — ten scores across two unrelated representations, none individually large, but not one of them positive. The descriptor-space distances, which encode metal and medium alongside the ligand, do order error, but weakly: the strongest reaches a rank correlation of $0.250 \pm 0.078$ against $0.648 \pm 0.020$ for model-derived uncertainty, and on a pair population held common to every score the best of them cuts retained-subset error by 50% at half retention where uncertainty cuts it by 65%. A ligand-space distance threshold applied to a structure-plus-context task is not the conservative default it is often taken to be; it is an unvalidated one, and on the evidence here it discards predictions no more error-prone than those it keeps.

The reason is visible in a score that uses no chemistry at all. Pair-density risk — a count of how many training ligands a metal pair carries, with no molecular structure and no model output — orders held-out error better than every distance we tested at every retention fraction from 30% upward, failing only in the extreme tail. How thinly a metal pair is sampled is a measured axis of difficulty on this data, and it is an axis no ligand-space geometry can see. Predictive uncertainty sees it without being shown it: as reported in Section 3.4, mean $\sigma_{\mathrm{total}}$ falls threefold across the pair-support bins although support is never a feature and never enters the loss.

For pair-specific extraction chemistry, then, selectivity should be modeled and evaluated directly. Pair-equal metrics expose behavior that row-weighted averages hide, and training on the pairwise target attaches the risk estimate to the quantity the experiment consumes; reconstructing it from two absolute-binding intervals costs more than a factor of two in calibration error. Two honest limits bound what that uncertainty can be asked to do. It is not a certificate of selectivity sign — three of every four nominal 90% intervals still straddle zero — and its coverage decays where support is thinnest, from $0.909$ on well-sampled metal pairs to $0.803$ on unseen ones. Its defensible role is to rank predictions by how much to distrust them, not to certify any one of them.

Turning that ranking into an acquisition rule is where the practical lesson lies, and it is about *how* uncertainty is used rather than whether. Two rules built from the same $\sigma_{\mathrm{total}}$ behave oppositely. Subtracting it as a lower confidence bound helps a near-zero search — event precision@1 of $0.802$ against $0.748$ for value alone — and destroys a high-selectivity one, falling from $0.823$ to $0.669$ and, at a shortlist of five, finding a qualifying ligand less often than random ranking. Dividing by it instead, as a standardized margin past the event threshold, gives the best event recovery on the high-selectivity objective *and* matches the best rule on the near-zero one, without ever dropping materially below the value-only baseline in either. The reason the two diverge is structural: $\sigma_{\mathrm{total}}$ grows with predicted selectivity, so a rule that subtracts it penalizes precisely the candidates a high-selectivity campaign exists to find, whereas a rule that divides by it asks the scale-free question of how confidently a candidate clears the bar. A single trade-off survives — value-only ranking still comes closest to the strongest available ligand, at the cost of finding qualifying ones less often — so the objective still has to be named. But the choice of functional form is the larger effect, and a standardized threshold margin is the uncertainty-aware default that does not need the objective known in advance.

Taken together these results argue for treating prediction risk as something to be measured on held-out data rather than assumed from chemical intuition — defining the novelty that matters, calibrating a risk score against outcomes, and testing rankings against the specific experimental objective in view. For sparse, context-dependent chemistry, that discipline is worth more than another decimal place of average accuracy, and it is what makes a selectivity model whose honest goodness-of-fit is barely measurable usable for deciding which experiment to run next.
